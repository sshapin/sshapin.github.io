In many different sports, one of the first things someone learns is how to fall safely. It is so important to hone this skill early, since most further training and execution occurs outside of padded spaces where spotters are present. This semester, I felt like I learned (and, in some ways, am still learning) how to properly take a fall in mathematics. 

I did not begin as a mathematics student in college. In fact, I spent my first two years of undergraduate as a computer science student in the College of Engineering chasing after my dreams of becoming a clarinetist and performing arts engineer. I enjoyed math, but hadn't paid it much mind, and I didn't come from a high school where it was ever emphasized as anything more than a set of tools you needed for engineering, or something, maybe. I came to math after I became increasingly interested in my job as a TA for a discrete mathematics course in the College of Engineering. Something about proofs felt satisfying, and it turned math into something more akin to a new field of art rather than a toolkit I would discover along the aisles of Home Depot. I spent that summer, between my Sophomore and Junior year, miserable as a budding software engineer and turning to math textbooks at the end of a stressful day at my internship as a means of relaxing. I began attending the Math department's walking club and meeting math professors and math students around that same time, and as I would meet individuals who were or were becoming mathematicians, I felt a longing to jump in. That following Fall semester, I crushed it in Math 217, a proofs-based linear algebra course which served as an onboarding course for math majors. The following semester, I took it up a notch and performed very well in Math 297, which was the "alternative route" Honors onboarding course. Neither of these courses were "easy" by any means, but I always felt like I was getting by, staying ahead, and that I wasn't facing any obstacles which would jeopardize my future standings. 

This semester doled out health challenges that I couldn't have predicted, and as a result, the first half of my semester turned into an academic mess. I failed an exam for the first time, and then I very nearly failed an exam for the second time. It wasn't because I couldn't understand the content. Rather, my time and ability to prepare for testing was all too finite. After I fully recovered, I made a comeback in many ways: I began asking more questions about the math I was learning and dug as deeply as I could into the content, and I raised my exam score significantly in the course for which I failed the first midterm. However, in my other math course, I was unable to catch my grade up to a point where I would end the class with a glowing A, despite my efforts. This is where I felt like I had truly taken my first fall in my math career. 

I am learning that I must take this in stride. My grade, an numbers-based view of my learning, is only one metric by which I can evaluate myself and my performance this semester. Due to those health challenges, I did have to cut back much of my original goals in the semester, and I'm stuck with the grades I have. However, none of this discounts how much I <i>did</i> get to learn over these past few months. I'll list them as follows, in no particular order:

- I connected with new professors and improved my ability to ask questions that fulfilled my curiosity about the math I was learning,
- I formed friendships which I cherish dearly with other math students and found a place for myself in the greater Michigan Math community,
- I vastly improved my self-studying abilities for mathematics, and now feel comfortable learning new topics on my own and engaging with the material with others, and
- I learned how to be more confident in myself and my abilities in front of others, and when having to face myself alone.

These things, I am learning, are tools and skills that I can utilize for the next time I stumble. My safe, but not necessarily painless, landing. I took a fall in that my performance this semester, numbers-wise, wasn't what I wanted it to be. But in doing so, I learned how to get back up and catch myself during future low points.
